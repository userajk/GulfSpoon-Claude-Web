34:T45c,At 200°C (400°F), medium asparagus about 1cm thick takes 6 to 8 minutes. Thin spears take 4 to 6 minutes and thick spears 8 to 10. Those times assume a preheated 5 to 6 litre basket holding about 450g (1 lb) in one layer.

Asparagus is green because of chlorophyll, and long cooking turns it a dull olive colour as the vegetable's own acids react with it. Short, hot cooking keeps the colour bright, which is why 200°C beats a gentler setting here. Only white asparagus, which is fibrous and needs peeling all the way down, does better at 190°C (375°F) for longer.

Does asparagus get crispy in the air fryer? The tips do, a little, and the stalks blister. The stalk itself stays tender rather than crunchy. For a crisp crust all over, use the parmesan or breadcrumb variation below, or wrap bundles of spears in strips of [halal turkey bacon](/recipes/air-fryer-turkey-bacon/) using the time in the chart.

Adjust for your machine. In a compact 3 to 4 litre basket, cook half a bunch at a time and start checking 1 minute early. In an oven-style air fryer with racks, use the upper rack and add 1 to 2 minutes.35:T45a,Sirloin is the best all-round choice: tender, beefy and cheaper than ribeye. Use any steak you would happily grill whole.

- **Sirloin.** Labelled "top sirloin" on American beef, "rump" on British and Australian beef. Lean, with enough flavour to stand up to garlic butter.
- **Striploin (New York strip).** A fat cap down one side; trim it before cubing or it stays chewy.
- **Ribeye.** The juiciest option. The fat marbling melts at 200°C, so it forgives an extra minute.
- **Tenderloin (fillet).** Very tender, but mild and expensive. Cut bigger cubes, 4cm, and keep it rare to medium.

Avoid stewing beef, chuck and pre-cut "beef cubes". They're full of connective tissue that only softens after an hour or more of slow cooking, so a few minutes in the air fryer leaves them tough.

Much of the fresh beef in Gulf supermarkets is imported and halal-certified, and the labels follow the exporting country's naming, so the same sirloin cut can be sold as "rump steak" in one shop and "top sirloin" in the next. If you're unsure, ask the butcher counter for a steak at least 3cm thick so the cubes come out even.36:T421,For 2.5cm cubes at 200°C (400°F), allow 5 to 6 minutes for medium-rare, 6 to 8 minutes for medium and 9 to 11 minutes for well done, shaking once at 4 minutes. Bigger 4cm cubes add about 2 minutes to each stage.

Those times assume a preheated basket and a single layer. A crowded basket cooks the steak in its own steam: the juices pool in the gaps, the surface stays grey and the inside overcooks while you wait for colour. If you see liquid collecting around the cubes after the shake, the batch was too big.

Small cubes carry very little heat over once they leave the basket, usually 1 to 2°C, so pull them at the temperature you want to eat. USDA guidance for whole cuts of beef is 63°C (145°F) followed by a 3-minute rest. Rare and medium-rare are below that and are a personal choice; serve medium or above to pregnant guests, young children and anyone with a weakened immune system.

Without a thermometer, press a cube with tongs. Medium-rare still yields, medium springs back, and well done feels firm. If all you have is large potatoes, cut them into even 2.5cm cubes and follow the same method: rinse, dry, oil, season, shake. Timings for every cut are in the chart, from whole mini potatoes to smashed. In Gulf supermarkets, baby potatoes often come in net bags with sizes from marble to almost 5cm. Spend a minute sorting them before you cut: leave the smallest whole, halve the middle sizes and quarter the biggest, so every piece is about the same thickness. For a sweeter side in the same basket, try [diced sweet potato with smoked paprika or cinnamon](/recipes/air-fryer-sweet-potato-cubes/).38:T4f4,Halved baby potatoes take 15 to 18 minutes at 200°C (400°F), and 2.5cm cubes of large potatoes take 18 to 22 minutes. Whole baby potatoes take longer, 20 to 25 minutes, because the skin slows moisture loss and heat has further to travel to the centre. That is also why whole ones come out creamy and soft while halves come out crisp.

Size matters more than variety. Every extra centimetre adds roughly 5 minutes. Baby gold potatoes are slightly drier and brown a touch faster; baby red potatoes are waxier and need a minute or two more before they feel tender.

Load is the other variable. Halves need a single layer. Cubes cope with two layers if you shake hard. Fill the basket to the brim and the top layer shields the bottom one, so you get pale, steamed potatoes underneath even after 30 minutes. For six people, two batches are faster in total than one crammed basket. Oven-style air fryers with trays sit the food further from the element, so add 3 to 5 minutes and swap the trays halfway.

For dinner from one machine, cook the potatoes first and keep them warm on a tray in a low oven, then use the hot basket for [crispy-skinned air fryer chicken thighs](/recipes/air-fryer-chicken-thighs/) or [salmon fillets with crisp skin](/recipes/air-fryer-salmon/).39:T43f,Any of them will cook well. They differ in texture, and in how well they survive being shaken.

- **Baby potatoes (gold, red or purple):** young, waxy and thin-skinned. Creamy inside and crisp on the cut face, with no peeling. The least work and the most reliable batch.
- **Yukon Gold and other all-rounders:** medium starch with a buttery interior. Cubes hold their shape, and the thin skin can stay on.
- **Russet and other floury potatoes (Maris Piper, King Edward):** high starch, low moisture. Cubes roughen and crisp hard on the outside and turn fluffy inside, but the corners can break when shaken. The broken bits go extra crunchy.
- **Large red-skinned and other waxy potatoes:** hold their shape but brown less and stay dense inside when cubed.

Loose potatoes in Gulf supermarkets are often labelled by country of origin rather than variety. To check, cut one in half and rub the faces together. If they stick and leave a white, starchy film, the potato is floury and will crisp well as cubes.
