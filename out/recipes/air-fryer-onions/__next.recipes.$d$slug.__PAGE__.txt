33:T419,Raw brats take 12 to 15 minutes at 190°C (375°F), turned once halfway. Thickness decides where you land in that range: a 3cm link is usually done at 13 minutes, a fat 3.5cm butcher's brat closer to 15. Chicken bratwurst cooks in about the same time as beef but must reach 74°C (165°F) rather than 71°C (160°F), so check it with a thermometer rather than going by colour.

At 200°C (400°F) raw brats are ready in 10 to 12 minutes, which is why many recipes use it. On thicker links, though, the casing tightens faster than the filling expands and often splits along one side, and juice runs out through the split. At 190°C that rarely happens.

Leave space between them. Brats that touch stay pale where they meet, because the hot air can't reach that strip of casing.

Oven-style air fryers with racks brown more slowly on the underside, so turn the brats twice instead of once and add 2 to 3 minutes. For more on why spacing and fan speed matter, see [how an air fryer actually cooks food](/beyond-the-recipes/how-does-an-air-fryer-work/).34:T568,**From frozen:** don't thaw them. Start raw frozen brats at 180°C (350°F) for 10 minutes, so the centre thaws before the casing over-browns, then turn them, raise the heat to 200°C (400°F) and cook for another 8 to 10 minutes. If the links are frozen together, run the pack under cold water for 30 seconds and prise them apart with a butter knife. Always confirm the centre temperature, because frozen brats vary more than fresh ones.

**Precooked or smoked brats** are already safe to eat, so you're only reheating and browning them: 6 to 8 minutes at 190°C (375°F) until they're steaming hot in the middle.

**Simmered first:** if you like a very juicy brat with a softer bite, simmer raw brats for 10 minutes in salted water or chicken stock with a sliced onion, keeping it just below a boil so the casings don't burst. Drain, pat them dry, then air fry at 200°C (400°F) for 4 to 6 minutes to brown the outside. Simmering is optional; the straight air-fryer method cooks them through safely on its own.

If you'd rather skip the peppers, cook the brats over thick [roasted onion wedges](/recipes/air-fryer-onions/) instead, using the same 5-minute head start. In the first few minutes they release a lot of liquid. If the basket is crowded or the mushrooms were salted, that liquid has nowhere to go and they simmer in it, turning grey and rubbery. With space around them and no salt yet, the moving air carries the water off quickly, and only then does the surface start to brown.

This recipe tosses the mushrooms in plain oil, roasts them for 8 minutes, and only then adds butter, fresh garlic and salt for a final 2 to 3 minutes. Fresh garlic needs about that long to soften and turn pale gold. Put it in at the start and it is black and bitter before the mushrooms are done.

Every Gulf country claims a version, and families argue over the details the way Italian families argue over Sunday ragù. The name comes from the Arabic word "kabsa" or "machboos" meaning "pressed," referring to the method of cooking rice pressed down with meat so the grains absorb every drop of flavor from the broth.

The dish traces its roots to the ancient trade routes that passed through the Arabian Peninsula. The core technique, cooking rice in a spiced meat broth, arrived from Persia and the Indian subcontinent centuries ago, but Gulf cooks made it their own through one ingredient that defines the entire cuisine: loomi, the sun-dried black lime. These small, hard, dark spheres are made by boiling fresh limes in salt water and drying them in the sun for weeks. The result is a flavor that does not exist anywhere else in cooking: sour, smoky, earthy, and faintly sweet all at once. It is the single ingredient that makes machboos taste like machboos and not like biryani.

In the UAE and Bahrain, machboos is typically made with chicken or lamb and served on a communal platter for Friday family lunches. In Kuwait, the version tends to be slightly wetter and more tomato-heavy. In Saudi Arabia, a closely related dish called kabsa uses a slightly different spice profile. But across the entire Gulf, the principle is the same: rice cooked in richly flavored broth, meat served on top, and the whole thing brought to the table on a single large platter meant to be shared.37:T41b,<p>Proper caramelised onions need long, gentle heat in a layer of their own juices, and an open basket dries them out before they get there.
